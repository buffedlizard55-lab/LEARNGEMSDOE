What the prize actually scores

A plain-language walkthrough of the problem, written from the official documents only. It is the executive summary of the reasoning, not of a result — no model has been trained here and no prediction exists.

This page is research narrative. It is not the submission narrative required by rules §3.2, which must accompany an actual submission and disclose generative-AI use. This repository's AI-usage log is the running record that such a narrative would be written from.

The geologic question

Geothermal systems in the Great Basin are commonly fault-controlled. Hermant and colleagues state it directly in the paper the competition itself cites: “most hydrothermal systems are fault-controlled”, so “accurate and complete fault mapping is therefore of prime importance for assessing the geothermal favorability of an area or a prospect” (Hermant et al., 2025).

The GEMS Prize asks for an algorithm that produces an enhanced fault map of the GeoDAWN region (competition hub). The wrinkle, and the whole difficulty, is in what “enhanced” is measured against.

Ground truth is split in two, and only one half is scored

Ground truth for currently known faults is public: the USGS Quaternary Fault and Fold Database and the INGENIOUS Great Basin Regional Dataset Compilation. Rules §3.3 confirms the training labels were obtained from the INGENIOUS compilation.

The scored target is the other half: “a set of newly identified faults labeled by geology experts at the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) and USGS” (rules §2). Those labels are private, fixed before the competition for Phase 1, and expanded by expert review of all submissions for Phase 2.

“Pixels corresponding to known USGS/INGENIOUS faults are masked / excluded from evaluation, so they do not count towards penalty terms. … The mask is indeed pixel-exact — it is identical to the provided set of training fault labels.” DrivenData staff, forum 11516

The consequence is easy to state and easy to forget while building: a model that reproduces the published fault map perfectly scores zero. Every pixel it gets right is a pixel the scorer removes.

The metric, and why it is lenient about false positives

The score is a distance-weighted Tversky index with α = 0.2 and β = 0.8, using a triangular kernel of 300 m support. Full definition and worked example: problem description. In words:

  • Missing a fault costs four times as much as a false alarm (β = 0.8 versus α = 0.2). Under-predicting is the expensive error.
  • Near misses are partly forgiven. A prediction 100 m from a ground-truth pixel keeps two thirds of its credit; at 300 m the credit reaches zero.
  • The kernel is asymmetric in one important way. It forgives false positives that are near new-fault ground truth. It does not forgive them for being near known faults: “A predicted pixel that is near a known fault trace but far from a new-fault ground truth pixel will be fully penalized.”

That asymmetry is the design intent of the whole prize. The rules and the forum answer together say: do not conservatively re-draw what is already mapped, and do not expect credit for hedging around it.

“New fault” is broader than it sounds

“For the purposes of this competition, ‘new fault’ means ‘any fault pixel not already captured by USGS/INGENIOUS’ and can include newly mapped geometry of an existing fault system.” DrivenData staff, forum 11536

So a continuation past a mapped endpoint counts. So does a splay, and a parallel strand in the same zone. Staff went further: a new-fault ground-truth pixel “can indeed lie within 300 m of a known fault trace”, and such pixels “would constitute corrections or modifications to existing fault traces”, with “identifying these corrections … one outcome we are aiming for as part of this competition”.

This is the portion of the target space that is officially confirmed to exist. It is also the portion a geologist would recognise immediately: mapping is better in some places than others, and endpoints are where mapping stops.

Why the catalogue is incomplete — the structural argument

The most useful thing in this project is not a model; it is the catalogues' own methodology. Three documented properties, each of which excludes a predictable class of real fault. Full sourcing in catalogue-gap reasoning.

  1. The database is defined by surface rupture. It contains faults that “demonstrate geological evidence of coseismic surface deformation” in the past 1.6 Ma (USGS faults page). A buried structure with no surface rupture is not an oversight — it is outside the definition.
  2. Compilation preferred published, topical, detailed-scale work. OF 93-338 told compilers to prefer “fault-related topical studies over general studies”, “more recent studies”, and “more detailed scale of work (i.e., 1:24,000-scale over 1:250,000-scale mapping)”. A scarp nobody has published cannot be compiled, however obvious it is in modern lidar.
  3. Inferred structures were deliberately down-weighted. The same guidelines: “suspected or inferred Quaternary faults (and folds) will be shown as dotted Quaternary structures … whereas structures with known late Tertiary (or older) movement will not be shown unless there is compelling evidence of Quaternary movement.”

And the region makes it worse. The competition's own About page: “Most faults in the GeoDAWN region of Nevada are more subtle, and many are hidden below the surface, requiring geophysical data to detect” (page 968). Hermant and colleagues quantify a second, different problem — heterogeneity — with a measured 400 m discrepancy between USGS traces and their own mapping in north-central Nevada, and fault density variation that is “not only due to the geological context”.

What the catalogue actually holds in the scored area (measured 2026-09-26, twice)

The argument above is structural. It is now paired with counts. Clipping both published compilations to the GeoDAWN data-extent polygon returns 413 INGENIOUS traces and 6,230.2 km of fault (CG-12) and 5,570 USGS sections and 6,241.3 km (CG-15). The two numbers describe the same fault: a 13.5× difference in record count against a 0.18% difference in length, agreeing to within 31.6 m everywhere. Record counts are granularity, not content.

Two things follow, and they pull in different directions. The scored area holds 413 of the 22,956 regional records (1.8%), so the labels are sparse — roughly 62,300 one-pixel-wide fault pixels against a 12,279,160-pixel grid, ≥ 0.51% positive (arithmetic from the two verified numbers, assuming a one-pixel-wide rasterisation). But the quality mix here is better than the regional mix: 1.5% of the USGS sections inside the footprint are Inferred against 23% regionally, certainty is Good for 99.9%, and every section is class A — the top evidence grade (CG-16). The GeoDAWN area is comparatively well mapped, and what is missing from it is not low-grade material that was excluded but high-grade material that never entered the literature (CG-13). inference for the interpretation; the counts are measured.

Compare Hermant et al.'s 6.5% positive fraction at 10 m with a 50 m buffer (GM-3): the imbalance here is worse by more than an order of magnitude, and their buffer is not available to us because the competition mask is pixel-exact (GV-5). That is the single most important structural fact about the scored problem.

What follows for method

Three consequences, each of which is a hypothesis card rather than a slogan:

  • Do not train on the catalogue as if it were the answer (H5). The official reference solution does exactly that (repository), and the mask makes it structurally futile. Hermant et al. reached the same conclusion and built their own labels, writing that the USGS mapping is “sometimes inaccurate at small scales or of variable precision”.
  • Look where cover is thick, and say so (H2). Band 15, depth to basement, is in the stack and costs nothing to condition on. Where cover is thick, the absence of a scarp is uninformative; where cover is thin, it is meaningful negative evidence.
  • Expect the gains near mapped faults, not far from them (H1). Corrections and extensions are confirmed to be in the label set. The deep unknown is real but unconfirmed, and staff have declined to say whether the labels include buried structures.

Underlying all three: validate on contiguous geographic blocks, not random patches. The reference solution uses random train/test splits; Hermant et al. used a geographic split and still report a large train-versus-validation gap (≈0.88 versus ≈0.59 PR-AUC on their better model). Random splits on a raster whose neighbouring patches share edges and acquisition artefacts will flatter any model, and this prize is specifically about generalising to ground truth nobody has seen.

Phase 2 is a different contest

Phase 1 scores against a fixed private set. Then an expert panel reviews every team's submission, verifies what is genuinely new, and all submissions are re-scored against the expanded set (competition structure). Staff made the strategic implication explicit when declining to describe the test faults: “the largest prize pool (Phase 2) will use a test set that is updated by expert review of all Phase 1 submissions, so your fault predictions have an impact on final evaluation even if they are not the most performant in Phase 1” (11527 post 7).

$250,000 of the $300,000 sits in that round. A submission that is geologically defensible — one where a reviewer can see why each candidate was proposed, what evidence supports it, and what was ruled out — is not just compliant, it is the product.
