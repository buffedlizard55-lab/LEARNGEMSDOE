Scarps, curvature, breaks-in-slope in Basin and Range terrain

DEM-based structural geomorphology

GM-1 · 1 m DEM is an official extra, not inside the 100 m stack

verified confidence: high

Problem description · rules §2 and §3.3 in NLR PDF

Claims: Feature GeoTIFF includes detrended elevation and slope of detrended elevation at 100 m. Separately, 1m_DEM_links.csv lists where 1 m DEM can be downloaded. Rules: feature data “also contain USGS Digital Elevation Model (DEM) elevation data at 1-m resolution.” About page: coordinated airborne lidar through USGS 3DEP over a similar extent as GeoDAWN.

Relevance: A 100 m pixel is coarser than many Basin-and-Range scarps. Catalogue compilers historically used mixed, often coarser, topographic bases (OF 93-338). 1 m lidar is the highest-leverage extra dataset for surface-scarp gaps — and the largest I/O cost.

Confidence: High. The CSV itself was not downloaded (login wall).

GM-2 · How mappers actually see Quaternary faults on DEMs (Hermant et al., 2025)

verified confidence: high for their protocol

Hermant et al., 2025, Stanford Geothermal Workshop

Claims: Many faults induce local topographic variations (fault scarps) mappable from elevation, slope, or satellite imagery. Authors used USGS 3DEP 1/3-arc-second (~10 m) DEM only where derived from lidar, because slope from non-lidar 3DEP was artefact-ridden. Visual criteria: generally straight line with strong contrast; different elevation on either side and steeper slope in the fault zone; vegetation contrasts; weathered rock/fractures. They applied a 50 m buffer around mapped traces because “the expression of a fault on the data can be thicker than the resolution of the data.” They note 1 m DEM would be heavier computationally; 10 m was a deliberate tradeoff.

Relevance: In this terrain, a “subtle scarp” is often a linear slope break, not a San Andreas-style ribbon. Buffering traces by tens of meters is standard when rasterizing. Competition rasterization is 100 m; DTI kernel is 300 m. A 1 m scarp that is real but only 1–2 pixels wide at 100 m is easy for a U-Net trained on thick catalogue traces to miss — or, conversely, to over-thicken.

Confidence: High for Hermant’s Nevada protocol. Not automatically the NLR/USGS private-label protocol (see CG-5).

GM-3 · Mattéo et al., 2021 — deep learning on optical + topographic fault mapping

verified confidence: medium (full text not fully extracted)

Mattéo, L., Manighetti, I., Tarabalka, Y., Gaucel, J.-M., van den Ende, M., Mercier, A., et al., 2021, Automatic fault mapping in remote optical images and topographic data with deep learning: JGR Solid Earth, 126, e2020JB021269. 10.1029/2020JB021269 (listed on the About page).

Claims: Title and venue confirmed. The paper addresses automatic fault mapping from remote optical images and topographic data using deep learning. Full methods/results were not line-extracted this session (Wiley page is long; paywall-adjacent).

Relevance: Organizer-endorsed prior art for DEM/optical fault mapping. Next pass should extract architecture, input bands, and reported error vs manual mapping.

Confidence: High that the paper exists at that DOI. Low on unquoted numerical results — none invented here.

GM-4 · QFFD geomorphic-expression field

verified

USGS OF 93-338

Claim: Compilers must describe geomorphic expression (fault scarps, offset streams, monoclines, shutter ridges, associated landslides, etc.). Example given: small (1–2 m) to moderate (<10 m) scarps on a lacustrine surface. Line type depends on continuity/surface expression; inferred traces are dotted.

Relevance: A 1–2 m scarp is invisible in a 100 m DEM slope but obvious in 1 m lidar. Catalogue completeness for small scarps is therefore a function of whether a topical study existed, not of whether the landform exists.

GM-5 · What a subtle or partly buried scarp looks like here — bounded statement

reasoning, not a new observation

No original DEM analysis was run (no 1 m tiles on disk). From GM-2 and the About page’s contrast with Carrizo Plain: expect degraded, dissected, or sediment-mantled slope breaks along range fronts and across piedmonts; intra-basin scarps may be only a metre or two high or expressed as vegetation lineaments (Hermant B8A rationale). Do not treat this paragraph as a field observation.
