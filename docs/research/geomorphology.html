DEM structural geomorphology

Two of nineteen bands are topographic, and both come from a 100 m detrended surface. That is the central tension in this domain: the scarps an expert would map are 1–10 m features, and the only topographic layers provided are an order of magnitude coarser than that. Everything useful here comes from knowing precisely where the coarse layer fails.

Entries

GM-1 What the two 100 m topographic bands can and cannot see

Source
Band tags: 12 “Detrended elevation — topography with regional trends removed”, 19 “Detrended elevation slope — gradient of elevation after detrending”, both category topographic
Citation
DrivenData reference-solution notebook band listing; see feature stack
What it claims
The elevation layer is detrended — a regional surface has been removed — and the slope band is the gradient of that detrended surface, not of raw elevation.
Why it matters here
Detrending is the right thing for scarp work: it removes the long-wavelength range-and-basin signal so local relief stands out. But a 100 m pixel averages 10,000 m² of ground. A 2 m-high scarp that is 20 m wide contributes a slope signal diluted roughly fifty-fold across the pixel that contains it, before any detrending error is considered. The practical consequence: bands 12 and 19 will resolve range-front scarps and large piedmont lineaments, and will miss the small, recent, low-offset scarps that are the most likely post-compilation discoveries. This is the direct justification for H3.
Confidence
verified Band tags. inference The resolution-dilution reasoning and the claim about which scarps survive are our analysis, not sourced statements.

GM-2 The 1 m DEM is official and provided as links, not as data

Source
Problem description — Provided features · Rules §3.3 · About page
Citation
DrivenData / DOE GEMS Prize problem description and official rules (September 2026)
What it claims
The problem description: “you will find a CSV file called 1m_DEM_links.csv that contains links where DEM data at 1m resolution can be downloaded.” Rules §3.3: “In addition, instructions will be provided for downloading USGS DEM elevation data at 1-m resolution for the GeoDAWN region”, and §2 states the feature data “also contain U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Digital Elevation Model (DEM) elevation data at 1-m resolution”. The About page adds that airborne lidar was collected through the USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) “over a similar extent spanned by the geophysical surveys”.
Why it matters here
The 1 m DEM is an officially sanctioned part of the feature set, not external data — so using it carries no licence question under the external-data rule. It is also the only provided layer at a resolution commensurate with the features experts map. Note the wording carefully: it is a CSV of links, so acquisition is a batch download job, and the About page says lidar covers an extent “similar to” — not identical to — the geophysical surveys. Lidar coverage is therefore itself a spatial variable, and coverage boundaries are exactly where catalogue completeness will change discontinuously.
Confidence
verified All three quotations read from the official pages this session.

GM-3 Hermant et al. (2025): a worked scarp-detection protocol for this exact region

Source
Stanford 50th Workshop PDF, cited by the competition's About page
Citation
Hermant, B., Kiersnowski, L., & Bellanger, M. (2025). Using deep learning to map Quaternary faults in Western USA. Proc. 50th Workshop on Geothermal Reservoir Engineering, Stanford University.
What it claims
Numbers read from the PDF:
  • Elevation from 3DEP 1/3 arc-second (~10 m ground spacing N–S; E–W varies with meridian convergence) — chosen because it is the highest-resolution seamless DEM with full conterminous-US coverage, and because it is lighter than a 1 m DEM.
  • “Clean slope raster data can only be calculated where elevation data is derived from LIDAR data.” They therefore used the 1/3 arc-second DEM only where it is lidar-derived, in northern central Nevada. Non-lidar 3DEP slope was artefact-ridden.
  • Their own manual label set: 1,100 faults, cumulative length 264 km, mapped over the eastern half of the study area, with the western half reserved for prediction.
  • Fault pixels buffered by 50 m → a 100 m-thick fault signature. 7,692 tiles retained after filtering to tiles containing at least one fault pixel; split 64 / 16 / 20 → 4,920 / 1,232 / 1,540, then augmented to 20,000 / 4,000 / 4,000.
  • Tiles 128 × 128 px at 10 m = 1,280 m across.
  • Fault pixels are 6.5% of the data after buffering. Loss: Focal Binary Cross Entropy with α = 0.065 (matched to the imbalance) and γ = 2.0.
  • Sentinel-2 B8A (20 m → 10 m, 865 nm, 20 nm bandwidth) chosen for vegetation/biomass sensitivity: “in areas of very flat topography where faults are not necessarily highlighted by elevation data but sometimes by vegetation orientation.”
  • Explicit caveat: in flat areas (basin centre, playa) their processing vertically exaggerates relief, which helps find structures but also exaggerates non-fault features — “Prediction results in flat areas should therefore be treated with caution.”
Why it matters here
This is the closest published analogue to the competition task, done in north-central Nevada, and it is cited by the organisers themselves. Five transferable lessons:
  • Lidar provenance gates slope quality. If you compute slope from non-lidar 3DEP, you get artefacts. That is a hard constraint on H3, and it means a lidar-coverage mask should be built before any slope processing.
  • 1,100 faults / 264 km gives a realistic prior for how much new fault length a manual expert pass adds in this terrain — i.e. how much signal a model could hope to find.
  • 6.5% positive pixels at 10 m with a 50 m buffer. At 100 m with a 1-pixel trace the positive fraction will be far smaller; the imbalance — and the focal-loss style fix — will matter more, not less.
  • Vegetation carries structural signal in flat terrain. No optical/vegetation band is present in the 19-band stack, so this is a genuine external-data opportunity (Sentinel-2 is open data).
  • Flat-area predictions are unreliable by the authors' own admission, and basins are exactly where cover-masked faults live. Their caveat is a warning against trusting the same failure mode in our output.
Confidence
verified Every number and quotation above was read from the PDF this session.

GM-4 Geomorphic expression is an admissible evidence class in the catalogue itself

Source
OF 93-338 · About page
Citation
Haller, Machette & Dart (1993), USGS Open-File Report 93-338
What it claims
OF 93-338 admits structures with pre-Quaternary movement only “unless there is compelling evidence of Quaternary movement (geomorphology, offset surficial deposits, etc.)”, and treats scarp morphology as supporting data for segmentation. The competition About page, describing how geologists detect faults, lists field searches for “surface expressions such as fault scarps, offset rock layers, and polished slickensides”, and from remote sensing “linear features and subtle ground movements indicative of fault activity”.
Why it matters here
Geomorphology is a valid route into the catalogue — but only when a human has already interpreted and published it. A scarp visible in lidar that nobody has written up is geomorphically identical to one that would qualify, and is absent from the catalogue purely for lack of publication. That is gap class 2 in CG-7, and it is the class most directly attacked by H3.

verified Quotations read from both official sources.

GM-5 The native detrended-elevation release

Source
10.5066/P9MQRCBY, listed as a resource of INGENIOUS GDR submission 1391
Citation
U.S. Geological Survey (2022). Elevation Trend and Detrended Elevation [Great Basin]. https://doi.org/10.5066/P9MQRCBY
What it claims
“Provided are maps of elevation trend and detrended elevation for the Great Basin, USA. The detrended elevation map … emphasizes local relative topogra[phy].”
Why it matters here
If bands 12 and 19 were resampled from this product, then the native grid, its documented detrending method and its resolution are all available as external data. That matters because detrending is not neutral: the wavelength of the removed trend determines which scarp heights survive. Knowing the trend model tells you which scarp scales the provided bands are blind to.

GM-6 What a subtle scarp looks like in this terrain — the operational picture

Source
Reasoning, assembled from GM-1 … GM-5 and the acquisition facts on feature stack. No single source states this.
Citation
—
What it claims
In the northwestern Great Basin, the fault expression most likely to be missing from the catalogue is a low-offset, basin-facing scarp on a piedmont or playa margin: a few metres of relief over tens of metres of width, continuous for hundreds of metres to a few kilometres along strike, often beheaded or dissected by alluvial channels, sometimes expressed only as a vegetation or tonal line. Operationally:
  • At 1 m lidar: a break-in-slope or a curvature maximum traceable along strike, possibly with a tonal/vegetation line (Hermant's B8A observation).
  • At 10 m 3DEP: visible only if lidar-derived; otherwise buried in artefacts (GM-3).
  • At 100 m bands 12/19: invisible individually, but a persistent along-strike run of slightly elevated slope may still register. Detection must be directional and multi-pixel, not per-pixel.
The confusers are specific and should be listed before modelling, not after: irrigation and drainage ditches, shorelines and wave-cut benches of pluvial lakes, alluvial-fan channel margins, lithologic benches, road and fence lines, and playa edges.
Why it matters here
This is the mental model a geologist reviewer will apply to a candidate map. Writing it down converts H3 from “use the 1 m DEM” into a testable detection problem with named false positives.

inference Deliberately unsourced beyond the components cited. This entry exists to be corrected by someone who has mapped these scarps in the field.

Location check. Figure 9 is the Leach Hot Springs area, printed with 40°36′N and longitudes 117°36′–117°42′W; Figure 8 (Rye Patch, western Humboldt Range) prints 40°30′–40°33′N. Why it matters here
The whole project rests on one premise: that faults exist which the catalogue lacks and which a geologist will confirm. Hermant et al. report exactly that outcome in this region, from lidar, with a U-Net-family model. It also gives a local scale for catalogue error: a >150 m positional discrepancy is half the 300 m scoring kernel, and the same order as the 400 m figure in CG-5.

GM-8 · The confuser list is observed, not invented

GM-8 Paleo-shorelines, canyons and streams were measured as false positives here

Source
Hermant et al. (2025), Figures 8–9 and §7, read this session.
What it claims
… both siUNET and FaultSEG models detect non faulted objects described by Silver et al. (2011), such as the paleo-shoreline or the Rec Area scarp.Figure 8 caption
… the detection of a canyon boundary (Figure 9, area B) and stream boundary (Figure 9, area A) raises questions as they are aligned with USGS Quaternary faults.§6

Why it matters here
Paleo-shorelines, canyon and stream boundaries are measured false positives in this terrain, and the authors warn that a dedicated confuser class can delete real faults because the two co-occur — the argument for soft suppression over hard negative classes (H11). Their deliberate over-prediction is also the right posture under α = 0.2 / β = 0.8.

GM-9 · The rules call the 1 m DEM feature data

GM-9 Officially provided, and officially in scope — plus a definitional discrepancy

Source
Rules §2 — https://www.nlr.gov/docs/fy26osti/96647.pdf (redirects to docs.nlr.gov), read this session.
What it claims
The feature data for this prize come from the recently released Geoscience Data Acquisition for Western Nevada (GeoDAWN) dataset, a high-resolution lidar, magnetic, and radiometric study of western Nevada and eastern California … In addition, the feature data also contain U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Digital Elevation Model (DEM) elevation data at 1-m resolution.Official Rules, §2 Background
Why it matters here
The 1 m DEM is described as feature data — in scope and officially provided, not external data needing a licence judgement. That strengthens H3 and removes the main objection to spending effort on lidar.
Irregularity flagged, not smoothed
The rules call GeoDAWN a "lidar, magnetic, and radiometric study"; the data release (Glen & Earney, 2024, 10.5066/P93LGLVQ) is titled "Airborne magnetic and radiometric surveys" and describes the lidar as a separate, coordinated 3DEP collection over "a similar extent". Two official DOE / USGS documents describe the same programme differently. No scoring consequence is known. Recorded so that nobody later cites the rules as evidence that GeoDAWN itself distributed lidar. See GV-13.

GM-10 · Hermant et al.'s own labels, and the four visual criteria they used

GM-10 Why the closest comparable study stopped using the catalogue, and how its mappers decided

verified verbatim §3.3 · the comparison to the competition is inference

Source
Hermant et al. (2025), §3.3 (read 2026-09-26).
Verbatim
As the USGS Quaternary fault mapping is sometimes inaccurate at small scales or of variable precision, we created a new manual mapping of faults over the eastern half of the study area … The remaining half was reserved for predictionHermant et al. §3.3
we created a fault label dataset containing 1100 faults with a cumulative length of 264 kmHermant et al. §3.3
What it means for this project
  • The precedent is exactly the shape of our problem: an expert remapped half the area, held the other half out, and only then trained and scored.
  • Count check. Their 1,100 labels over half the area are more labelled features than the entire USGS compilation holds inside the same half (CG-15: 5,570 sections over the whole footprint, mean 1.12 km each). So the expert label set is a different granularity from either compilation — which is what the competition's new-fault labels are too.
  • Their cumulative 264 km is ~4% of the 6,230 km of catalogue trace inside the footprint. Their labels are not the catalogue at higher resolution; they are a different, much smaller, locally-verified set.
The four visual criteria, verbatim in substance
  • a straight line with strong contrast;
  • an offset in elevation with a steeper slope on one side;
  • a variation in vegetation;
  • geological features such as weathered rock or fractures.
Why the criteria matter more than the counts
They are a human decision rule, and every one of them is a surface signature. That is the strongest independent support in the library for GM-2's claim that elevation and imagery carry the signal, and it explains GM-8's confuser list from the other side: “a straight line with strong contrast” is satisfied by roads, canals, fence lines and drainages. Any confuser channel (H11) has to be built from exactly those non-fault causes of the same four criteria.
How to close the gap
Nothing to close — the section is read. What remains is GM-9: matching their 264 km against our own label raster once it is placed, which is the only local truth set available (see H10 and PA-8).

Blocked-on, and what to do the moment data land

  1. Read 1m_DEM_links.csv and count tiles, coverage and total volume before committing to H3.
  2. Build a lidar-coverage mask from the 3DEP tile index for the region and intersect it with the GeoDAWN survey outline. Coverage boundaries are where catalogue completeness should jump — test that directly.
  3. Check whether bands 12/19 match the detrended-elevation release at 10.5066/P9MQRCBY once resampled. If they do, the detrending method is documented and the blind spots become computable.
  4. Do not compute slope from non-lidar 3DEP. Hermant et al. tried it and found it artefact-ridden; that is a published negative result and it is free to respect.
