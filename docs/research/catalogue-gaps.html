Catalogue-gap reasoning

The scored target is not “what looks fault-like”. It is “what the catalogue does not contain”. That makes the catalogues' own methodology the most valuable document in this project: if you know how a fault had to be evidenced to get into the database, you know exactly which faults were structurally incapable of getting in.

The one-sentence version. The USGS database exists to archive faults with geological evidence of coseismic surface deformation, compiled from published literature, with a stated preference for topical studies and detailed mapping scales, and — since 2017 — with a reduced metadata set. Every one of those four properties excludes a specific, predictable class of real fault. Those classes are the target.

CG-1 The database is defined by surface rupture, not by structure

Source
USGS — Quaternary Fault and Fold Database of the United States
Citation
U.S. Geological Survey, 2020, Quaternary Fault and Fold Database for the Nation, https://doi.org/10.5066/P9BCVRCK
What it claims
“This database contains information on faults and associated folds in the United States that demonstrate geological evidence of coseismic surface deformation in large earthquakes during the past 1.6 million years (Ma).” The page explains the 1.6 Ma cutoff is inherited from the 1983 Geologic Time Scale in use when the database was established in 1993; the Quaternary has since been redefined several times, most recently to 2.58 Ma, but the database retains the original definition. The USGS FAQ states the same limit more bluntly: “A Quaternary fault is one that has been recognized at the surface and that has moved in the past 1,600,000 years (1.6 million years).” (What is a Quaternary fault?, read 2026-09-26.)
Why it matters here
This is the design limit that creates the target class. A buried or blind structure with no demonstrable surface deformation is not merely unmapped — it is out of scope by construction. The same is true of a structure whose most recent deformation is older than the window, and of one where surface rupture has not been demonstrated even though the structure is real. Combined with the About page's statement that in the GeoDAWN region “many are hidden below the surface, requiring geophysical data to detect” (page 968), this is the strongest available argument for hypothesis H2.

CG-2 1993 compilation rules: what a compiler was told to include and exclude

Source
Haller, Machette & Dart, 1993, Open-File Report 93-338 — Guidelines for U.S. Database and Map
Citation
Haller, K.M., Machette, M.N., and Dart, R.L. (1993). Maps of Major Active Faults, Western Hemisphere International Lithosphere Program (ILP) Project II-2: Guidelines for U.S. Database and Map. U.S. Geological Survey Open-File Report 93-338.
What it claims
The guidelines told compilers to “give preference to (1) fault-related topical studies over general studies (i.e., those addressing paleoseismology versus general geology), (2) more recent studies, and (3) more detailed scale of work (i.e., 1:24,000-scale over 1:250,000-scale mapping)”. On inferred structures: “suspected or inferred Quaternary faults (and folds) will be shown as dotted Quaternary structures (age category 5), whereas structures with known late Tertiary (or older) movement will not be shown unless there is compelling evidence of Quaternary movement (geomorphology, offset surficial deposits, etc.)” OF 93-338, Introduction The document also notes the compiler “is forced to select a slip rate and age even when no data may exist”, and that a fault should only be treated as segmented when it “should have been well studied … in a paleoseismologic sense (trenching and dating)”.
Why it matters here
Three separate exclusion mechanisms, all documented:
  • Evidence must be published. A scarp nobody has written up cannot be compiled, no matter how visible it is in lidar. That is precisely the class of thing 3DEP lidar made visible after the compilation.
  • Inferred structures are deliberately down-weighted or dropped. A geophysically inferred buried fault with no surface expression has no route into the database.
  • Scale heterogeneity is baked in. Areas mapped at 1:24,000 were preferred over areas mapped at 1:250,000, which produces mapping-density differences that reflect publication history rather than geology.
This is the methodology foundation under hypotheses H1, H2 and H3.

verified Quotations read directly from the official USGS PDF this session.

CG-3 Since 2017 the database is maintained with a reduced attribute set

Source
USGS faults page, and the database-search retirement notice on the same page
Citation
U.S. Geological Survey, 2020, 10.5066/P9BCVRCK
What it claims
“In order to maintain a current and relevant dataset, effective January 12, 2017, the USGS will maintain a limited number of metadata fields that characterize the Quaternary faults and folds of the United States. Archived reports are accessible from the abbreviated record.” Separately, the page states that as of 26 February 2026 the Database Search function is retired and legacy reports are reachable through the interactive fault map.
Why it matters here
Two consequences. First, maintenance capacity has been deliberately reduced, which is consistent with a compilation whose completeness reflects when an area was last actively worked on rather than how many faults it contains. Second, the retired search function is a practical, time-sensitive note for anyone planning to pull per-fault metadata: the route in is now the interactive map at 10.5066/F7S75FJM, and the GIS zip (Qfaults_GIS.zip, 16 MB).

CG-4 INGENIOUS Qfaults: an update of the USGS attributes, not a new mapping

Source
Geothermal Data Repository — submission 1391
Citation
Ayling, B., Faulds, J., Morales Rivera, A., Koehler, R., Kreemer, C., Mlawsky, E., Coolbaugh, M., Micander, R., dePolo, C., Kraal, K., Wagoner, N., Siler, D., DeAngelo, J., Glen, J., Peacock, J., Batir, J., Gentry, E., Berti, C., Lifton, Z., Clark, A., Kirby, S., Hardwick, C., and Kleber, E. (2022). INGENIOUS — Great Basin Regional Dataset Compilation [Data set]. Geothermal Data Repository, GBCGE, NBMG, UNR. https://doi.org/10.15121/1881483
What it claims
The compilation's own descriptions: Quaternary Faults v1 is a “Shapefile (NAD83 Geographic) containing updated quaternary fault traces, ages, and slip rates for the INGENIOUS study area. Attributes conform to USGS Qfault Database s[tandards]”. Quaternary Faults v2 “contains an updated version of the INGENIOUS quaternary fault compilation shapefile and text document with field definitions. It supersedes 'Quaternary Fau[lts v1]'”. Licence: CC BY 4.0. The whole compilation is 9 files, 116.98 MB.
Why it matters here
The training labels come from this compilation (rules §3.3), so this is the label set the scorer masks out. Two things follow. It is an attribute update of USGS traces — traces, ages, slip rates — which means it inherits the USGS inclusion criteria documented in CG-1 and CG-2 rather than fixing them. And v2 supersedes v1, so any outside copy of the fault shapefile must be checked for version; the field-definition text document in the v2 archive is the authoritative field dictionary.
Corroboration from the project's own team
Hart-Wagoner, Coolbaugh, Faulds & Mlawsky (GBCGE / Nevada Bureau of Mines and Geology, University of Nevada, Reno), Feature Engineering of Fault Attributes for Play Fairway Analysis, Great Basin Region, USA, state that “A Quaternary fault database for the INGENIOUS GBR was collated in Phase I of the INGENIOUS project and included updated fault locations and updated fault attributes of recency (i.e., age of most recent rupture) and slip rates.” That is an independent, project-internal confirmation of what v1's own description implies: the compilation updated locations and attributes, and did not re-map from new evidence. Project context from the INGENIOUS project page: PI Bridget Ayling, Co-PI James Faulds; 1 February 2021 – 30 June 2025; $10,000,000 under DOE Geothermal Technologies Office award DE-EE0009254.

CG-5 Measured heterogeneity and up to 400 m of positional misfit

Source
Hermant, Kiersnowski & Bellanger (2025), Stanford 50th Workshop on Geothermal Reservoir Engineering — cited by the competition's own About page
Citation
Hermant, B., Kiersnowski, L., & Bellanger, M. (2025, February). Using deep learning to map Quaternary faults in Western USA. Proceedings, 50th Workshop on Geothermal Reservoir Engineering, Stanford University.
What it claims
Figure 2 caption: “USGS Quaternary fault density map highlighting the regional variation in Quaternary fault mapping that is not only due to the geological context … (Right) Local discrepancy observed in North Central Nevada: distance between USGS Quaternary faults and TLS fault label can be up to 400 m.” The text attributes heterogeneity to “the aggregation of different mapping sources or scales in this database” and notes that “some areas where the geology has been mapped more precisely have an artificially higher fault density and accuracy than areas where geological and structural mapping has been done at a larger scale.”
Why it matters here
The 400 m figure is larger than the metric's 300 m kernel radius. That is the quantitative core of hypothesis H1: if published traces can sit up to 400 m off the true scarp, then ground-truth pixels for “new” faults can be up to 400 m from the nearest catalogue pixel — which is exactly what staff confirmed is possible and even desired (forum 11516 post 4). It also means a naive “stay near the known line” prior is not safe.
Confidence
verified Figure caption and surrounding text read from the PDF. Note the measurement is in north-central Nevada, inside but not coextensive with GeoDAWN; treat it as regional, not area-specific.

CG-7 The gap classes, derived

Source
Derived from CG-1 … CG-6. Not a sourced claim in its own right.
Citation
—
What it claims
Combining the documented inclusion rules with the known data history gives four predictable gap classes:
  1. Corrections to known traces — tip extensions, splays, parallel strands, and positional errors up to ~400 m (CG-5). Staff have explicitly said these count.
  2. Post-compilation visibility — scarps that only became mappable with 3DEP lidar / GeoDAWN-era elevation, i.e. after the topical literature that the compiler was told to prefer (CG-2).
  3. Cover-masked structures — real faults with no demonstrable coseismic surface deformation, which CG-1 places outside the database's definition entirely.
  4. Low-publication terrain — areas where no topical study at detailed scale was ever published, so the compiler had nothing to prefer (CG-2 rule 3).
Why it matters here
Each class maps onto a different feature signal: class 1 and 2 onto topography (bands 12, 19, plus 1 m lidar), class 3 onto gravity and magnetics under thick cover (bands 5, 11, 13, 15, 18), class 4 onto nothing structural and everything independent — strain, seismicity, conductivity (bands 4, 7, 8, 10, 16, 17). That mapping is the bridge from this domain to the hypothesis cards.
Confidence
inference This entry is reasoning, not sourced fact. It is written to be argued with by a geologist, which is the point.

What would falsify this domain's working assumption

  • If the private label set turns out to be dominated by long, obvious, entirely unmapped range-front faults, then the “corrections and subtle scarps” framing in CG-7 is wrong and detection should be biased toward large, continuous, high-amplitude lineaments.
  • If a spatial holdout shows that distance-to-known-fault is a positive predictor of new-fault labels, the gap is diffuse rather than adjacent, and H1 should be demoted relative to H2/H4.
  • If the INGENIOUS v2 field definitions reveal per-fault provenance or mapping-scale attributes, CG-7 class 4 could be measured directly instead of inferred. Partly resolved 2026-09-26: the live NBMG service exposes FTYPE_ and MAPSCALE (CG-8). The remaining test is a clip to the GeoDAWN outline, which has not been run.

CG-10 · The catalogue is a literature compilation, not a survey

CG-10 Completeness tracks publication and state-survey capacity

Source
USGS Quaternary Fault and Fold Database page — "Background" and "History", read 2026-09-26.
What it claims

Verbatim:

This database was used to create the fault-source characterization in the National Seismic Hazard Maps … For the hazard maps, both the fault surface trace and the metadata are simplified representations of the geometry and behavior of the fault, based on geologic interpretation.USGS, Quaternary Fault and Fold Database page
Starting in the early 1970s, mainly in response to national concerns about the siting of nuclear reactors, scientists needed to locate active and Quaternary faults and document their characteristics.same page, "History"
These map compilations, however, did not provide much supporting data. Subsequent state-scale compilations, such as those by Johns and others, (1982), Stickney and Bartholemew (1987), and Hecker (1993) provided some supporting database and were the first true fault compilations.same page
In 1993, the U.S. Geological Survey began developing a database for Quaternary faults and folds for the United States in earnest, largely supported by NEHRP but with significant support from many State surveys.same page
Why it matters here
Completeness tracks what was published and which state survey had capacity, not the actual fault population. Joined to CG-2 — compilers were told to prefer published, recent, detailed-scale topical studies — this is the mechanism that produces CG-7 class 4. A fault in an unfashionable quadrangle is absent for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it exists.

verified — read from the USGS page this session.
