Potential-field geophysics

Ten of the nineteen bands are potential-field or derived from them: six magnetic, four gravity. This is the only family in the stack that responds to structure under cover, which makes it the only family that can see the gap class the USGS database excludes by definition (see catalogue-gap reasoning, CG-1 and CG-7 class 3).

PF-1 What the GeoDAWN surveys can and cannot resolve

Source
USGS GeoDAWN data page · 10.5066/P93LGLVQ
Citation
Glen, J.M.G., and Earney, T.E. (2024). GeoDAWN: Airborne Magnetic and Radiometric Surveys of the Northwestern Great Basin, Nevada and California. U.S. Geological Survey data release, https://doi.org/10.5066/P93LGLVQ. CC0 1.0.
What it claims
149,030 line-km over 51,857 km², flown 1 Nov 2021 – 20 Nov 2022. Area 1 (Clayton Valley, Li-brine focus) at rank-1 EarthMRI specs: 200 m flight lines, 2,000 m tie lines, 100 m clearance over low relief. Area 2 (geothermal focus, between rank 1 and 2): 400 m flight lines, 4,000 m tie lines, 150 m clearance. Magnetic processing included diurnal correction, aircraft-field correction, tie-line levelling, micro-levelling and IGRF removal. Helicopter over the Tonopah block, fixed-wing elsewhere.
Why it matters here
Flight-line spacing sets the shortest magnetic wavelength that is sampled rather than aliased. At 200 m line spacing the reliable content is coarser than 200 m along the N–S tie direction but ~400 m or coarser across it in Area 2, before you resample to a 100 m grid. A lineament you “find” at 100 m pixel scale may be interpolation between lines 400 m apart. Treat any magnetic edge shorter than roughly the line spacing with suspicion, and test it — that is hypothesis H7.
Confidence
verified Acquisition numbers quoted from the official USGS page. inference The resolution/aliasing implication is standard potential-field practice, not a statement in the source.

PF-2 What is actually in the magnetic bands

Source
Band description / data_category tags printed by the official reference notebook; band list on the feature stack page
Citation
DrivenData (2025), gems-prize-reference-solution, notebook cell output. https://github.com/drivendataorg/gems-prize-reference-solution
What it claims
Six magnetic bands: 1 magnetic anomaly, 2 reduced-to-pole, 3 TMI horizontal gradient, 6 “tilt angle or total curvature — magnetic field derivative for edge detection”, 9 TMI vertical gradient, 14 TMI.
Why it matters here
Three observations, each with a consequence:
  • Band 6 is already an edge detector. The derivative work has been done for you. Whether it is a tilt derivative or a curvature is genuinely ambiguous from the tag — “tilt angle or total curvature” is the tag's own wording — and the difference matters, so this should be checked numerically against bands 3, 9 and 14 once data are placed (a tilt angle is bounded and zero-crossing based; total curvature is not).
  • Bands 3 and 9 together give the full horizontal gradient magnitude and, with 14, the analytic-signal amplitude. These are the classic edge detectors; none of them requires a scarp.
  • RTP is provided, so you should use band 2 rather than band 1 or 14 for anything geometric. At mid-latitudes an unreduced anomaly peak is displaced from its source; RTP removes that displacement. The database's own About page describes aeromagnetics as providing “key information on … subsurface structure and geology”.
Confidence
verified Band tags. inference Identification of band 6 as tilt vs curvature, and the recommendation to prefer RTP.

PF-3 The tilt derivative — Miller & Singh (1994)

Source
Journal citation, corroborated across multiple citing references: https://doi.org/10.1016/0926-9851(94)90022-1
Citation
Miller, H.G., & Singh, V. (1994). Potential field tilt — a new concept for location of potential field sources. Journal of Applied Geophysics, 32, 213–217. 10.1016/0926-9851(94)90022-1
What it claims
The tilt angle is the arctangent of the ratio of the vertical derivative to the horizontal gradient magnitude. Its value is bounded, so it responds to shallow and deep sources with comparable amplitude, and its zero contour sits over source edges — which is why it became the standard edge locator in potential-field work.
Why it matters here
The amplitude-balancing property is exactly what you want in a basin-and-range setting, where a deep basement step under several hundred metres of fill produces a weak, broad gradient that a plain gradient-magnitude filter suppresses next to a shallow, sharp anomaly. If band 6 is a tilt angle, it is the single most gap-relevant band in the stack.
Confidence
verified Bibliographic record (title, journal, volume, pages, DOI) corroborated. unverified The method description above is the standard textbook form; the paywalled article itself has not been opened in this project. Do not quote this entry as a verbatim statement of the authors.

PF-4 Total horizontal derivative of the tilt — Verduzco et al. (2004)

Source
https://doi.org/10.1190/1.1651454
Citation
Verduzco, B., Fairhead, J.D., Green, C.M., & MacKenzie, C. (2004). New insights into magnetic derivatives for structural mapping. The Leading Edge, 23(2), 116–119. 10.1190/1.1651454
What it claims
The paper extends the tilt derivative by taking the total horizontal derivative of the tilt angle, producing sharper, better-localised maxima over source edges than the tilt zero-contour alone, and it discusses the analytic-signal amplitude alongside it.
Why it matters here
This is the method to apply on top of band 6 if band 6 is a tilt angle, or to compute from bands 3, 9 and 14 if it is not. It is cheap: a few gradient operations on grids that already exist, no external data, no reprocessing of line data. That makes it the lowest-cost experiment available for hypothesis H2.
Confidence
verified Bibliographic record corroborated across independent citing sources. unverified The paywalled article has not been opened here; the summary is the conventional description of this method, not a quotation.

PF-5 The gravity family, and the regional maps behind it

Source
Stack band tags · Glen et al., 2022, regional Great Basin gravity and magnetic maps · INGENIOUS compilation
Citation
Glen, J.M.G., et al. (2022). Regional geophysical maps of the Great Basin. U.S. Geological Survey data release, https://doi.org/10.5066/P9Z6SA1Z
What it claims
Four gravity bands: 13 isostatic gravity anomaly, 5 its slope, 11 its vertical gradient, 18 its horizontal gradient. The INGENIOUS record describes the source release as “Regional geophysical maps of the Great Basin, USA … generated from new and existing sources to support ongoing efforts to characterize geothermal resource potential”.
Why it matters here
Gravity is independent evidence from magnetics: different physical property, different failure modes. A lineament that appears in both band 18 (gravity horizontal gradient) and band 3/6 (magnetic) is far harder to explain away as lithology or acquisition artefact. That conjunction is the natural definition of a high-confidence H2 candidate. The regional maps at 10.5066/P9Z6SA1Z give the native-resolution context for what the 100 m bands were resampled from.
Confidence
verified Band tags and the INGENIOUS description of the release. unverified That the competition's gravity bands derive from this specific release is not stated anywhere official — the composition of the feature stack is undocumented beyond the band tags.

PF-6 Band 15, depth to basement — the cover axis

Source
Band tag: “Depth to basement surface — thickness of sedimentary cover”, category subsurface
Citation
DrivenData reference-solution notebook band listing (see PF-2)
What it claims
Per-pixel thickness of sedimentary cover over the magnetic basement surface.
Why it matters here
This is the stratifying variable for the whole catalogue-gap argument. Where cover is thick, the absence of a mapped surface scarp is uninformative — a buried fault can be real, geophysically visible, and structurally excluded from QFFD. Where cover is thin or absent, a missing scarp is meaningful negative evidence and a magnetic edge more likely reflects lithology than structure. Any H2 candidate should be reported conditioned on band 15, not as a flat map. This is the cheapest single piece of conditioning available and it costs nothing, because the band is already in the stack.
Confidence
verified Band tag. inference The conditioning strategy is our reasoning, not a sourced claim. The band's own provenance, method and error are undocumented.

PF-7 Artefacts that will masquerade as structure

Source
Acquisition facts from PF-1; reasoning ours
Citation
—
What it claims
Three artefact families are predictable from the acquisition design and should be controlled for, not ignored:
  1. Mosaic discontinuity. Area 1 (200 m lines) abuts Area 2 (400 m lines). Apparent lineament density and gradient amplitude will change across that boundary for purely acquisitional reasons.
  2. Block boundaries. Four acquisition blocks — Winnemucca, Fallon, Hawthorne, Tonopah — flown by different contractors and aircraft. Levelling joins can leave residual line-to-line or block-to-block offsets that gradient filters amplify into straight “structures”.
  3. Terrain clearance. The USGS page notes that in steep terrain “the aircraft may have required deviating from the planned drape surface, and therefore variable terrain clearance should be considered when modeling and interpreting these data”. Variable clearance changes anomaly amplitude independently of geology.
Why it matters here
All three produce linear, fault-shaped features. A model that learns them will produce confident, plausible, entirely false predictions — and because β=0.8 punishes misses more than α=0.2 punishes false positives, the metric gives you latitude to over-predict, which makes this failure mode easy to fall into and hard to notice on the public leaderboard. The control is cheap and public: the GeoDAWN release ships Esri shapefiles of the flight paths and survey outlines. Overlay them and reject any candidate lineament that parallels a survey or block boundary — that is H7.
Confidence
verified Acquisition facts, block names and the terrain-clearance quotation. inference The claim that these artefacts will appear in the derived bands is reasoning; it has not been measured here.

PF-8 · What "rank 1" certifies — and what it does not

PF-8 Rank is a sampling certificate, not a quality adjective

Sources
GeoDAWN text naming the criteria — USGS data page and ScienceBase item. The criteria themselves — Drenth, B.J., & Grauch, V.J.S. (2019), Finding the Gaps in America's Magnetic Maps, Eos (2019), doi:10.1029/2019EO120449 (Table 1 read). The criteria as re-stated by USGS for the national survey inventory — Airborne Geophysical Survey Inventory metadata.
What it claims

Verbatim, GeoDAWN: Area 1 "was flown with rank 1 specifications (following criteria outlined by Drenth and Grauch, 2019) that met EarthMRI survey requirements"; Area 2 used "lower resolution flight specifications … (falling between rank 1 and 2)".

Verbatim, Table 1 (survey-specifications row), the definition of rank 1: "TC < 152 meters and ratio ≤ 2", where TC is terrain clearance and the ratio is "of flight line spacing to typical distance above shallowest magnetic sources in the survey area". Ranks run 1 (best) to 5 (worst), and the overall rank is the worst of three categories: data type, survey specifications, data issues.

Why it matters here
GeoDAWN Area 1 is 200 m line spacing at ~100 m clearance, so rank 1 certifies a spacing-to-source-distance ratio ≤ 2 — the survey is designed to image sources no shallower than about half the line spacing. Area 2, at 400 m spacing, is explicitly below that standard. Two consequences for H2: (a) a buried structure expressed only in the shallowest magnetic layer may be undersampled in Area 2 by design; (b) gridding to 100 m does not create information that 400 m sampling never captured — the 100 m product grid oversamples Area 2 across strike. (a) is a documented specification; (b) is the standard sampling argument, inference.
Confidence
verified for the GeoDAWN wording, the Table 1 criteria and the Eos DOI. unverified for the Eos byline in full — the USGS survey-inventory metadata credits the ranking to Drenth and Grauch (2019), the article credits "coauthor Tien Grauch" and an image to Benjamin J. Drenth, and the complete author list was not captured. The resolution consequences are inference.

PF-9 · Acquisition geometry has an azimuth, and the structures do too

PF-9 Flight lines run E–W; sampling is anisotropic by a factor of ten

Source
USGS GeoDAWN data page / ScienceBase item, read 2026-09-26.
What it claims

Verbatim, Area 1: "Flight lines were spaced 200 m apart at an azimuth of 90 degrees, and tie lines were spaced 2000 m apart at an azimuth of 180 degrees." Area 2: "flight lines spaced 400 m apart at an azimuth of 90 degrees, and tie lines spaced 4000 m apart at an azimuth of 180 degrees."

Why it matters here
Flight lines run E–W (090°), tie lines N–S (180°), so sampling is anisotropic by a factor of ten (200 m vs 2,000 m in Area 1; 400 m vs 4,000 m in Area 2). A N–S-trending structure is crossed every 200–400 m; an E–W-trending one is crossed by tie lines only every 2–4 km. Basin-and-Range normal faults trend broadly N–NE (well sampled); Walker Lane dextral structures and many range-front splays trend NW to WNW (obliquely to poorly sampled). Any comparison of "how strong does this lineament look" across orientations compares different sampling densities unless it is corrected for.
Confidence
verified for the azimuths and spacings. The orientation-specific sampling consequence and the regional trend generalisation are inference — the trend claim is a half-hour measurement against trace orientations in the label raster once data are placed, and should not be quoted until it is made.

PF-10 Area 1 / Area 2 flight specifications, contractor scope, and the deliverables that would settle band provenance

verified from the data page · the consequences for band numbering are inference

Source
USGS GeoDAWN data page, read in full 2026-09-26. Glen & Earney 2024, 10.5066/P93LGLVQ.
Verbatim, the parts that constrain the bands
  • Extent: four blocks (Winnemucca, Fallon, Hawthorne, Tonopah) totalling 149,030 line-km over 51,857 km²; published bounding box −120.0024, 37.3641, −116.1415, 40.7247 (WGS 84), consistent with the outline polygons used for the census (CG-9, CG-12, CG-15).
  • Area 1: 100 m mean terrain clearance over the valleys, 150 m over the mountains, 200 m line spacing, 2,000 m tie-line spacing.
  • Area 2: 150 m / 200 m clearance, 400 m line spacing, 4,000 m tie spacing.
  • Contract: EDCON-PRJ, 1 November 2021 – 20 November 2022, with subcontractors for the radiometric processing.
  • Deliverables, verbatim: “geoTIFF images of geophysical grids”, plus processed profiles, line location data, the contractor's report and metadata.
What it settles
  • Azimuth, corrected 2026-09-26 (this entry previously mis-stated the verified text). The data page specifies, verbatim, flight lines at an azimuth of 90° and tie lines at 180° for both Area 1 (200 m / 2,000 m spacing) and Area 2 (400 m / 4,000 m) — the geometry behind PF-9. An earlier rendering of this entry claimed “Area 1 lines flown at 22° (NNE); Area 2 at 310°”; that sentence is unsupported by the page and contradicts PF-9’s verbatim quotation, and is removed. The 22° figure that page does contain is the maximum climb/descent angle of the drape surface, a different quantity. The correction is recorded in the changelog rather than silently reversed.
  • Two different surveys with different specifications feed the same raster. Area 1 is finer and lower; Area 2 is coarser and higher. PF-7's seam question is therefore not hypothetical — there are four blocks and two flight geometries inside one grid.
  • A band-provenance route now exists. Because the release ships geoTIFF grids and a contractor's report, the notebook's 19 band tags can be checked against the delivered grids and processing descriptions without touching the competition raster. This is the cheapest way to close GV-17 and to confirm which bands are raw data and which are derived.
What it does not settle
The page does not enumerate the 19 bands, does not state the grid cell size or datum of the delivered geoTIFFs, and does not name the derived products (depth-to-basement, curvature, distance-to-earthquake). Those must come from the delivered files or the contractor report.

Cheapest next experiments, in order

  1. Confirm numerically what band 6 is: compare it against atan2-style tilt computed from bands 3, 9 and 14, and against curvature. Ten minutes once data are placed.
  2. Overlay the GeoDAWN survey-outline shapefile and test whether band-6 / band-3 maxima cluster along the Area 1 / Area 2 boundary and block boundaries (H7).
  3. Stratify every magnetic and gravity edge candidate by band 15 (cover thickness) and report detection statistics per cover class.
  4. Compute the analytic-signal amplitude and the tilt's total horizontal derivative, and test whether either adds information over band 6 alone against held-out catalogue faults — a sanity check before trusting it on unknowns.

PF-11 · Horizontal-gradient and tilt derivatives: priority vs. reality

PF-11 HGM / TDR / analytic signal — what exists, what is claimed, what is still unmeasured

band tags + DOIs verified · derivative definitions inference until the papers are read · sibling feature lists are site claims

Source
Band tags as printed by the reference notebook (see the feature stack); Miller & Singh (1994), 10.1016/0926-9851(94)90022-1 (record verified, paper unread); Verduzco et al. (2004), 10.1190/1.1651454 (same qualification); sibling sites 6GEMSDOE / GEMSDOE4 methodology notes (read 2026-09-26).
What the priority asks
Horizontal-gradient magnitude (HGM) maxima sit over susceptibility/density contrasts; the tilt derivative normalises the vertical derivative by the horizontal gradient so shallow and deep edges plot comparably; the analytic-signal amplitude is magnetisation-direction tolerant. Introduced for edges by Miller & Singh and popularised by Verduzco et al. — formula-level content is deliberately not reproduced from memory; both papers are on the unread list.
What already exists (verified)
Band 6 is tagged “Tilt angle or total curvature — magnetic field derivative for edge detection”, a band carries the vertical slope of total magnetic intensity, and band 15 is a depth-to-basement surface. Exactly one magnetic edge derivative ships; the gravity anomaly appears raw and as slope only.
What the sibling field implements (site claims)
6GEMSDOE lists derived HGM, analytic-signal amplitude, tilt derivative, multi-scale curvature, break-in-slope and structure-tensor lineaments (88 channels); GEMSDOE4 lists 63 lineament features on six edge-bearing bands. Neither build is held here; recorded as published text.
Open, score-relevant questions
  1. If band 6 is the TMI tilt derivative, re-deriving it is redundant; the marginal channels are gravity HGM/TDR (basement density edges under cover) and the analytic signal. Unmeasured — rasters not placed.
  2. Cross-layer edge coincidence (magnetic edge + gravity edge at one line) is the discriminator between a buried fault and a lithologic contact; no single-band edge detector can supply it.
  3. Derivatives amplify line-parallel acquisition noise (PF-9); the H7 artefact screen must run after derivation, not before.
Confidence
verified for the band tags' existence as printed and the two DOIs; inference for the definitions and the redundancy/confluence arguments; sibling feature lists are site claims.
