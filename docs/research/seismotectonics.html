Seismotectonics and strain

Seven bands in this family — three geodetic, two seismic, one conductivity, plus depth-to-basement borrowed from potential-field. None of them is a fault detector. All of them are independent of the published fault map, which is exactly why they are useful: they can point at a place where the crust is demonstrably doing something and the catalogue says nothing.

The disciplining rule for this whole domain. Strain and seismicity fields are smooth and regional. Faults are thin and local. A high strain-rate invariant does not tell you where a fault is, only that deformation is happening nearby. Use these bands as spatial priors that re-weight candidates produced elsewhere, never as a standalone probability map — unless you want a confident, diffuse, high-false-positive output. That is written into hypothesis H4 as a rejection criterion.

ST-1 The three geodetic strain bands

Source
Band tags: 4 “Geodetic second invariant — measure of strain rate tensor magnitude”, 7 “Geodetic shear rate — rate of angular deformation from GPS/InSAR”, 8 “Geodetic dilatation rate — rate of volumetric strain (expansion/contraction)”. All category geodetic_strain.
Citation
DrivenData reference-solution notebook band listing; see feature stack
What it claims
The three standard invariants of a 2-D horizontal strain-rate tensor derived from geodetic observations: the second invariant (overall magnitude), the shear component (angular distortion), and the dilatation (area change).
Why it matters here
These are the only provided bands that measure present-day deformation directly. Deformation does not require a published scarp. A pixel with elevated second invariant and no catalogue fault within 300 m is a defensible statement that the catalogue is silent where the crust is not — the “kinematic orphan” of H4. Note also the decomposition: dilatation (band 8) is the extensional signal, which in this province is associated with opening and therefore with permeability, so it carries geothermal relevance as well as structural relevance.
Confidence
verified Band tags and category. unverified The underlying velocity field, its station density, the smoothing applied, and the reference frame are all undocumented in the competition materials.

ST-2 The two seismic bands are parameterised, and not independent of each other

Source
Band tags: 10 “Distance to earthquake (n=100km radius, a=15° azimuth parameters)”, 16 “Earthquake intensity or density (n=100km radius, a=15° parameters)”. Both category seismic.
Citation
DrivenData reference-solution notebook band listing
What it claims
Two seismic fields computed with the same neighbourhood and azimuth parameters — a 100 km radius and a 15° azimuth term.
Why it matters here
Two consequences, one practical and one methodological:
  • They are not independent evidence. Both derive from the same catalogue with the same smoothing parameters. Counting them as two confirmations of the same hypothesis is double counting.
  • A 100 km smoothing radius is very large relative to a 300 m scoring kernel. A density band built with a 100 km neighbourhood is a regional field; it cannot localise a fault to within 300 m. It can tell you a fault is plausible in a broad area.
Treat bands 10 and 16 as one weak regional prior, not two.
Confidence
verified Band tags including the parameter strings. inference The non-independence and over-smoothing conclusions follow from those parameters; they are stated nowhere official.

ST-3 The strain–earthquake-rate relationship, in the peer-reviewed literature

Source
10.1785/0220220153
Citation
Kreemer, C., & Young, Z.M. (2022). Crustal Strain Rates in the Western United States and Their Relationship with Earthquake Rates. Seismological Research Letters, 93(6), November 2022. https://doi.org/10.1785/0220220153. First author affiliated with the Nevada Bureau of Mines and Geology and the Seismological Laboratory, University of Nevada, Reno. Author list confirmed from the INGENIOUS project page.
What it claims
Presents crustal strain rates for the western United States and examines their relationship to earthquake rates.
Why it matters here
The relevant point is the imperfect correlation between geodetic strain and seismicity. Where the two agree, you have two independent lines of evidence for active deformation and stronger grounds to suspect an unmapped structure. Where they disagree — high geodetic strain with low seismicity, or vice versa — the disagreement itself is informative: it may indicate aseismic creep, a short observational window, or strain distributed across structures too small or too slow to appear in either the seismic record or the fault catalogue. The last case is precisely the catalogue-gap target. Kreemer is also a co-author on the INGENIOUS compilation that supplies the training labels, so the strain products in the stack plausibly sit in this lineage.
Confidence
verified Title, journal, volume, date, DOI and first-author affiliation confirmed from the publisher record. unverified The abstract body has not been read — only the title/record is confirmed — so no quantitative claim from the paper is reproduced here. inference The lineage link to the stacked strain bands is our suggestion, not a sourced fact.

ST-4 Native geodetic and seismic products, free of the 100 m resampling

Source
INGENIOUS GDR submission 1391
Citation
Ayling et al. (2022), 10.15121/1881483, CC BY 4.0
What it claims
Two downloadable archives: Geodetic Shear and Dilation Models (51.99 MB) — “GeoTIFFs and accompanying ASCII, .csv, and metadata that describe geodetic shear and dilation models for the INGENIOUS study area, developed at the Nevada Geodetic Labo[ratory]”; and Earthquake Density Models (22.98 MB) — “GeoTIFFs and accompanying ASCII, .csv, and metadata that describe independent and dependent earthquake density for the INGENIOUS study area, developed at the Nevada Geo[dettic Laboratory]”.
Why it matters here
Two concrete gains. First, the archives ship metadata, so the smoothing, station network and construction of the strain and density fields become knowable — which is exactly the information missing from the 100 m bands (ST-1). Second, the earthquake density release distinguishes independent and dependent density, i.e. declustered vs. raw. For locating structures rather than aftershock clouds, the declustered field is the right one, and knowing which is which matters. Both are CC BY 4.0, so both satisfy the external-data licence condition.
Confidence
verified Descriptions quoted from the official GDR record. unverified The archive contents have not been downloaded or read; nothing here is asserted about the internal files.

ST-5 Conductivity: one surface band here, five depth slices in the source release

Source
Band 17 “Conductivity surface — electrical conductivity of subsurface”, category subsurface · Peacock & Bedrosian (2022) conductance maps
Citation
Peacock, J.R., & Bedrosian, P.A. (2022). Electrical conductance maps estimated from a 3D model of the Great Basin, USA. U.S. Geological Survey data release, https://doi.org/10.5066/P9TWT2LU, via GDR 1391
What it claims
The stack carries a single conductivity-surface band. The release listed in the INGENIOUS compilation “presents electrical conductance maps estimated from a 3D model of the Great Basin, USA, at five different depth ranges, spanning 2 to 200 km depth”.
Why it matters here
Conductance is sensitive to fluids, clays and alteration along structures, so a conductive lineament can corroborate a candidate fault that has no surface expression. But it is equally sensitive to basin fill, salinity and geothermal outflow, none of which is a fault. Used as a primary detector it will light up exactly the flat, sedimentary, low-lying areas where Hermant et al. warn that predictions should be treated with caution. Used as a multiplier on a candidate produced by structure-aware evidence, it is cheap and occasionally decisive. The depth slicing in the source release is the improvement worth having: a conductor at 2 km means something different from one at 50 km, and the single stacked band cannot say which.
Confidence
verified Band tag and the release description. unverified That band 17 derives from this release is not stated officially.

ST-6 Slip and dilation tendency — kinematic plausibility of a candidate

Source
10.5066/P9YL58W6, listed in GDR 1391
Citation
Siler, D.L. (2022). Shapefile for slip tendency and dilation tendency calculated for Quaternary faults in the Great Basin. U.S. Geological Survey data release, https://doi.org/10.5066/P9YL58W6. Author and title as listed on the INGENIOUS project page.
What it claims
“This database contains the results of slip tendency and dilation tendency analysis of Quaternary faults in the Great Basin region” — i.e. how favourably each known fault is oriented for slip or dilation in the regional stress field.
Why it matters here
Note the limitation: it is computed for known Quaternary faults, so it cannot discover anything on its own. Its value is as a plausibility filter on candidates — given a proposed strand with an orientation and dip, is that orientation one that the regional stress field would actually activate? That is a genuinely geological test rather than a statistical one, and it is the kind of argument a Phase 2 reviewer can engage with. It also serves geothermal relevance directly, since dilation tendency relates to permeability.
Confidence
verified Description quoted from the official GDR record. unverified The release has not been downloaded; no field-level claim is made.
