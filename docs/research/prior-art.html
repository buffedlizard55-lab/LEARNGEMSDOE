Prior art

Two published efforts map faults with deep learning in this province, and the organisers cite both. One of them is also the official baseline. Knowing what each was actually optimised for tells you what it will get wrong here — and the thing they have in common is that neither was optimised for finding faults the catalogue lacks.

Entries

PA-1 Mattéo et al. (2021) — the citation the organisers give first

Source
10.1029/2020JB021269 · listed on the competition's About page
Citation
Mattéo, L., Manighetti, I., Tarabalka, Y., Gaucel, J.-M., van den Ende, M., Mercier, A., Tasar, O., Girard, N., Leclerc, F., Giampetro, T., Dominguez, S., & Malavieille, J. (2021). Automatic fault mapping in remote optical images and topographic data with deep learning. Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth, 126(4), e2020JB021269. https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JB021269
What it claims
A deep-learning approach to automatic fault mapping from remote optical imagery and topographic data. Confirmed from the publisher record: open access under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, JGR Solid Earth volume 126 issue 4, corresponding author L. Mattéo (Université Côte d'Azur, Géoazur).
Why it matters here
It is the first of only two papers the organisers point competitors at, so it defines the expected frame of reference for a Phase 2 reviewer. The relevant structural point is the input modality: optical imagery plus topography. The 19-band stack has no optical band at all, so the modality the organisers' first citation uses is the one modality this competition does not provide — which is itself an argument for bringing Sentinel-2 in as external data (see feature stack, and Hermant's use of B8A in GM-3).

PA-2 Hermant et al. (2025) — closer analogue, with a published failure mode

Source
Stanford 50th Workshop PDF
Citation
Hermant, B., Kiersnowski, L., & Bellanger, M. (2025). Using deep learning to map Quaternary faults in Western USA. Proc. 50th Workshop on Geothermal Reservoir Engineering, Stanford University.
What it claims
Design, as read from the PDF: two U-Net-family models — siUNET (487,297 parameters) and a deeper in-house FaultSEG with roughly 100× the parameters. Three-channel input (elevation, slope, Sentinel-2 B8A) at 10 m, 128 × 128 tiles. Labels: 1,100 manually mapped faults, 264 km cumulative, 50 m buffer, over the eastern half of the study area with the western half reserved for prediction. After filtering to tiles containing fault pixels: 7,692 tiles, split 64/16/20 into 4,920 / 1,232 / 1,540, then augmented (Albumentations) to 20,000 / 4,000 / 4,000. Fault pixels are 6.5% of the data. Loss: Focal Binary Cross Entropy with α = 0.065, γ = 2.0. Metrics: PR-AUC and weighted Focal IoU. Reported learning curves reach PR-AUC ≈ 0.88 on training and ≈ 0.59 on validation for FaultSEG at epoch 17.5; siUNET ≈ 0.56 / 0.47.
Why it matters here
Three lessons, and one of them is a warning:
  • The generalisation gap is the story. Train PR-AUC 0.88 against validation 0.59 for the better model is a large gap, on a geographic split — training on one half of the area, predicting on the other. They did the split the hard way and still report a substantial gap. Any local split that is merely random will look better than it is.
  • They trained on their own labels, not on the USGS catalogue. Their stated reason is exactly our problem: “As the USGS Quaternary fault mapping is sometimes inaccurate at small scales or of variable precision, we created a new manual mapping.” That is a published endorsement of the core argument in hypothesis H5 — do not train to reproduce the catalogue.
  • Their output is a fault map, not a gap map. Nothing in their design distinguishes catalogue faults from non-catalogue faults. Fine for their purpose, orthogonal to ours.
Confidence
verified All design numbers and the learning-curve values were read from the PDF this session. The overfitting reading is our interpretation of their own reported train/validation curves.

PA-3 The official reference solution (Lipor)

Source
github.com/drivendataorg/gems-prize-reference-solution — README and notebook
Citation
Lipor, J. (2025). Reference solution for the GEMS Prize Challenge. DrivenData. Portland State University.
What it claims
The notebook's own approach summary: “an ensemble of U-Net models trained across multiple train/test splits”; the steps are “(1) Load and pre-process multi-channel geospatial features … and fault labels; (2) Split feature and fault label rasters into patches; (3) Train U-Net models on patches with data augmentation, applying random train/test splits for cross-validation, and using a Tversky loss function weighted to penalize false negatives more than false positives; (4) Average predictions from best model across each iteration for final output.” Implementation uses segmentation_models_pytorch and torchvision.transforms.v2; device selection is CUDA → MPS → CPU. Local filenames expected are data/numeric_features.tif and data/labels.tif. Preprocessing: values below −1e38 set to NaN, then each channel min-max normalised to [0, 1] over non-NaN values.
Why it matters here
This is the baseline the leaderboard will be full of, so beating it is the practical bar. Its Tversky weighting (β > α) correctly mirrors the competition metric's asymmetry. Everything else about it should be treated as a starting point, not a template — see PA-4.

PA-4 Where the reference likely falls short of the scored target

Source
Reasoning from PA-3 plus the official scoring clarifications · forum 11516 post 4 · forum 11536
Citation
—
What it claims
Four structural mismatches between the baseline's design and what is actually scored:
  1. It is trained to reproduce the labels that scoring removes. The training target is the USGS/INGENIOUS raster; the mask that excludes catalogue pixels from scoring is “identical to the provided set of training fault labels” (staff, 11516 post 4). A model that reproduces the training raster perfectly would score zero. It can only help by generalising off the ink, which is an accident of the architecture rather than an objective.
  2. Random train/test splits leak spatially. The notebook states random splits for cross-validation. Patches drawn at random from the same raster share edges, noise and acquisition artefacts; neighbouring patches are not independent samples. Reported cross-validation performance will overstate generalisation to unseen ground — the exact failure Hermant et al. measured at 0.88 vs 0.59 on a proper geographic split.
  3. The 1 m DEM is unused. The reference reads numeric_features.tif only. The officially provided 1 m DEM — the layer at a resolution commensurate with the scarps experts map — is not in the baseline at all. That is a free, sanctioned, unused source of signal.
  4. Nothing is conditioned on cover thickness. Band 15 (depth to basement) is in the stack, but a single model applied uniformly cannot express the fact that “no scarp” means something different under thick fill than on bedrock. The catalogue-gap argument is fundamentally conditional on cover; the baseline is not.
Why it matters here
Each mismatch is also an opportunity, and three of the four are cheap to fix without new data: change the split to a spatial holdout, add a lidar-derived channel, and stratify or condition on cover thickness. Only the first — the training target — requires rethinking what a positive example is, which is hypothesis H5.
Confidence
verified The staff quotations and the notebook's own statement about random splits. inference The four mismatches and the claim about leakage are our analysis. Item 3 depends on the notebook cells not read (see PA-3) — if later cells do load DEM tiles, this item is wrong and should be struck.

PA-5 The organisers' own menu of methods

Source
About page — About the task
Citation
DrivenData / DOE GEMS Prize, problem page 968
What it claims
“Geologists detect and delineate faults using a combination of traditional field observations, geophysical surveys, and advanced remote sensing and imaging techniques.” On computation: “modern computational methods including edge detection, Hough transforms, and deep learning on seismic or topographic datasets further enhance automated fault mapping.” And on this region specifically: “Most faults in the GeoDAWN region of Nevada are more subtle, and many are hidden below the surface, requiring geophysical data to detect.”
Why it matters here
The organisers are telling competitors that non-deep-learning lineament methods are in scope, and they are explicitly saying that in this region the interesting faults are subtle and hidden. A submission that is purely a U-Net trained on the catalogue is answering the weaker half of that sentence. A classical edge-detection pass (see the tilt-derivative entries) is cheap, explainable to a geologist reviewer, and targets the hidden half directly — and explainability is what Phase 2 review runs on.

verified All three quotations read from the official About page.

PA-6 · Hermant et al. (2025): the numbers the paper states

PA-6 PR-AUC 0.902 train / 0.595 test — and overfitting from epoch 7

Everything below is stated in the paper's prose (§6), not read off a chart. Source: Stanford PDF.

What it claims
  • Setup (verbatim): "The siUNET and FaultSEG models were both trained on the same augmented dataset with the same hyperparameters (Adam optimizer, 20 epochs and a learning rate of 10⁻⁵)."
  • PR-AUC: FaultSEG 0.902 train / 0.610 validation / 0.595 test. siUNET 0.574 / 0.463 / 0.449.
  • Weighted Focal IoU (verbatim): "a threshold ≤ 0.3 should be considered as good performance for FaultSEG (≥ 0.879, ≥ 0.594 and ≥ 0.580 for training, validation and test respectively) and ≤ 0.2 for siUNET (0.600, 0.498 and 0.453 …)".
  • Overfitting (verbatim): "the loss and metrics show a clear deviation between the values for training and the validation datasets after 7 epochs … This overfitting is also present but to a lesser extent for siUNET after 12 epochs." FaultSEG at 7 epochs still outperforms siUNET at 12.
Refines PA-2 — a correction worth stating plainly
The epoch-resolved figures recorded in PA-2 (PR-AUC ≈ 0.88 / ≈ 0.59 for FaultSEG; ≈ 0.56 / ≈ 0.47 for siUNET) were approximations taken from a figure whose axis labels were truncated in our extraction, and PA-2 cites them at "epoch 17.5", which the prose does not support. They are consistent with the values above but they are not the paper's stated numbers. Use the values in this entry. The per-epoch table (Figure 7) has deliberately not been transcribed, because its column headers did not survive extraction and transcribing half a header row is how wrong numbers enter a knowledge base.
Why it matters here
The generalisation gap is the whole story: 0.902 in training against 0.595 in test, on the better of two purpose-built models. Any score quoted on a random-patch split is not comparable to this. Note the absolute level too — PR-AUC ≈ 0.6 out of sample for a bespoke fault detector on 10 m lidar-derived inputs is the realistic ceiling to plan around, not a floor to beat on the first attempt.

PA-7 · Published design advice for exactly this problem

PA-7 "Any part of the study area may contain some faults, even if they are not in the label"

Source
Hermant et al. (2025), §7, read this session.
What it claims
… the fault labels used for training are inherently uncertain as manual fault maps are never exhaustive.§7, opening the three difficulties
This highlights the need to inform the model that any part of the study area may contain some faults, even if they are not present in the fault label. This could be done by including an adversarial loss term (Goodfellow et al., 2015) or by introducing noise in the true label (Mnih and Hinton, 2012), which will be the subject of further work.§7
It has been decided to take the risk of predicting too many faults, even if this means deleting them manually afterwards with an expert analysis, rather than missing or incorrectly mapping certain faults.§7
[W]e believe that iterative learning …, using faults predicted by the model and validated by an expert as fault label, can improve the qualitative and quantitative detection performance …§7
Why it matters here
Region-specific, published endorsement of three assumptions this project already makes: (1) the catalogue is an incomplete sample, so training to reproduce it encodes the wrong target; (2) under α = 0.2 / β = 0.8, over-prediction is the correct posture; (3) expert re-labelling of model output is the improvement mechanism — which is precisely the Phase 2 structure on the problem page. It is the strongest outside support for H9.
Caveat to carry
Their own models overfit after 7 epochs on 20,000 augmented tiles at 10 m. Porting the recipe to 100 m inputs with a smaller positive fraction is not free.

verified.

PA-8 · An independent lidar-mapped fault set inside the GeoDAWN box

PA-8 Silver et al. (2011) — western Humboldt Range, lidar and hyperspectral

Citation
Silver, E., MacKnight, R., Male, E., Pickles, W., Cocks, P., & Waibel, A. (2011). LiDAR and hyperspectral analysis of mineral alteration and faulting on the west side of the Humboldt Range, Nevada. Geosphere, 7(6), 1357–1368. doi:10.1130/GES00673.1
What is verified
The DOI resolves to that title in Geosphere vol. 7 no. 6, p. 1357 (checked 2026-09-26). The full citation is printed in Hermant et al. (2025)'s reference list, which is where it was found.
Why it matters here
Hermant et al. use it as the reference fault mapping for the Rye Patch / western Humboldt Range area, and their models' false positives include features "described by Silver et al. (2011), such as the paleo-shoreline or the Rec Area scarp". If that mapping can be obtained, it is an independent lidar-derived fault set inside the GeoDAWN bounding box — a local truth set for asking "does this detector find faults the catalogue lacks?" without spending a submission slot or touching private labels. That is H10.

Synthesis: what a better design would look like

  1. Change the split before changing the model. Hold out contiguous geographic blocks, not random patches. Until that is done, every number in this project is optimism.
  2. Score with the real metric, on the real mask. scripts/metrics.py implements the distance-weighted Tversky index with a pixel-exact known-fault mask so local holdouts behave like the official scorer.
  3. Positives must be defined by something other than the catalogue. Morphological extensions, geophysical edges under cover, lidar scarps — all measured off the mask.
  4. Keep one classical, explainable branch alive. A tilt-derivative / gradient-magnitude lineament pass costs hours, not weeks, and it is the part a geologist can argue with.
  5. Report conditioned on cover thickness and lidar coverage. Both are knowable; both change what absence of evidence means.
